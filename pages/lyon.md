##Patrick Lyon

###Introduction

Blacksmith Patrick Lyon constructed iron doors for the Bank of Pennsylvania in the summer of 1798, but his involvement with the bank was far from over (Avery). When the bank was robbed just weeks later, he was a prime suspect ensnared in a lengthy investigation and sentenced to Walnut Street Prison (Lyon). Neither his story nor his incarceration ended when the robber, Isaac Davis, confessed and returned the money, though (Lyon; Avery). Despite his innocence, he remained imprisoned for the next few months — bank authorities insisted that he must have been an accomplice to the crime, even as the evidence indicated otherwise (Lyon). While in prison, he started recording his experience, and soon after the charges against him were dismissed, he published The narrative of Patrick Lyon, who suffered three months severe imprisonment in Philadelphia gaol; on merely a vague suspicion, of being concerned in the robbery of the Bank of Pennsylvania: with his remarks thereon (Lyon; Conway 31–41).

Throughout his [memoir,](https://link.gale.com/apps/doc/CB0129560326/ECCO?u=nclivedc&sid=gale_marc&xid=b6fd6063&pg=2) Lyon traces his connections to the Bank of Pennsylvania, beginning with a book vault job he completed in 1797 and continuing on to his accusation, efforts to exonerate himself, and time in the Walnut Street Prison. His work illuminates the harsh reality of imprisonment — even in a prison praised for its reform efforts — and the unequal nature of the justice system. Though he was innocent, as a blacksmith and recent immigrant, he could not afford his high bail or the finest lawyers and was at the mercy of the wealthy and powerful bank administrators fighting against him. While he eventually got out  — and even received $9,000 in damages for false imprisonment in a [Supreme Court case](https://link.gale.com/apps/doc/U0102966360/MOME?u=nclivedc&sid=summon&xid=e8314823&pg=1) years later — he remained committed to his conclusion: the justice system is not truly just (Lloyd). It prioritizes those who can afford to manipulate it rather than the truth.

{% include feature/image.html objectid="15" width="75" caption="The cover of Lyon's memoir." %}

Lyon’s reflections on innocence, class, and the justice system stretch far beyond his narrative. His commentary is relevant today, when many innocent people are incarcerated and even sentenced to death (“Death Penalty”). While the context has evolved, similar patterns still unfold, like systemic bias and official misconduct (“Death Penalty”). Addressing these enduring issues is a crucial step toward reforming the carceral state and promoting true justice, and engaging with Lyon’s story is a meaningful way to start.

###Background and Accusations

While this work centers on Patrick Lyon’s involvement with the Bank of Pennsylvania, his blacksmithery began long before. Born and raised in London to Scottish parents, he began mechanical study at just 11 years old (Avery; Lyon 5). He continued to develop his skills over the next 14 years by working in manufactories throughout London (Lyon 5). However, Lyon craved more opportunity and “resolved to come to America” to find it (Lyon 5). Despite his family’s and friends’ displeasure, he arrived in Philadelphia in November 1793 (Lyon 5).

Lyon’s adjustment to America was not seamless. He first found work with a man named Samuel Wheeler, who cheated him of his wages and stifled his ingenuity (Lyon 5). Though this exploitation colored his early time in Philadelphia, it did not hinder his professional progress. Lyon soon began his own business, where he specialized in hydraulic engines and proved his technical skills by introducing at least twelve new kinds of iron manufacture (Lyon 6; Powell 37). Ironically, this success contributed to his eventual incarceration — he was recommended to work for Bank of Pennsylvania, the organization that imprisoned him despite his innocence (Lyon 6).

At first, the Bank of Pennsylvania employed Lyon to furnish its book vault (Lyon 7). He completed this work in May 1797 and returned to the bank for another project the following summer: finishing iron doors (Lyon 7). Yet Lyon was concerned about the security of these doors, especially for “such vast property” (Lyon 7). He expressed these anxieties to Samuel Robinson, the leader of the project, but Robinson seemed to brush them aside; that was no surprise to Lyon, though (Lyon 7-8). He was accustomed to his superiors disregarding him, even writing that “if you attempt to describe the best mode of working, it is but very seldom listened to” in his narrative (Lyon 8). For Lyon, being both doubted and belittled due to his status was not the exception; it was the rule. 

That same pattern played out later that summer, when Lyon was commissioned to install iron doors for the bank’s move into Carpenter’s Hall (Lyon 8-9; Avery). Once again, Lyon worked thoroughly and told Robinson that the locks they were using were not secure enough (Lyon 8-9). However, his diligence was received poorly. Robinson criticized Lyon for the extensive time he had spent on the project, claiming that he took too long; he even told him that the bank refused to work with him again (Lyon 9). Despite that mistreatment, Lyon remained invested in the project. He noticed Robinson and a strange man, who had been in his shop earlier, drinking together on Market Street a few weeks later; they became skittish upon seeing Lyon, so much so that Lyon told his apprentice that he did not “think they [were] after any good” (Lyon 11). His commitment to his work remained steadfast, even after his negative experiences.

{% include feature/image.html objectid="11" width="75" caption="James Akin's engraving of Lyon in Walnut Street Prison." %}

Meanwhile, yellow fever had been spreading throughout the city. Philadelphians had evacuated for their safety, and Lyon soon did the same, bringing his apprentice, James McGinley, along with him (Lyon 8; Lyon 11). He could not outrun the disease, though: McGinley soon fell ill, and despite medical attention, he died of yellow fever just days after leaving the city (Lyon 14-15). While Lyon survived, his circumstances did not improve. About a week after McGinley’s death, Lyon learned that the Bank of Pennsylvania had been robbed and immediately suspected Robinson and the stranger he had seen with him (Lyon 15). He discussed the event with an acquaintance, Mr. Hunter, just a few days later. Hunter admitted that many innocent people had been accused, but failed to mention the most relevant piece of information until Lyon pressed him: Lyon was one of the men on that list (Lyon 15-16).

Upon learning that he was a suspect, Lyon rushed to Philadelphia to prove his innocence. He even felt faint with anxiety when bank president Mr. Fox and city alderman Mr. Stocker examined him, but that worked against him. Fox and Stocker read it as an indicator of guilt, not as a reflection of innocent nerves (Lyon 20). These authorities further belittled him when Lyon asked why they accused him rather than his fellow blacksmith. Fox and Stocker argued that Lyon worked suspiciously slowly, framing his commitment to his craft as a cause for concern rather than an admirable quality (Lyon 20). Despite Lyon’s best efforts to assert his innocence, his fate was sealed — he was a suspect destined for solitary confinement in Walnut Street Prison.

###INCARCERATION EXPERIENCE

Philadelphia’s Walnut Street Prison, where Lyon was imprisoned, holds a special status in carceral history. Built in 1773 and nicknamed “the cradle” of the American penitentiary, it inspired several American prisons’ structures and systems (Benson; Roberts and Roberts). The prison was also an early target for reform efforts — the administration allegedly focused on more humane treatment and rehabilitation rather than punishment (Benson; Lownes 10–13; Turnbull 4). Yet Walnut Street Prison’s history is far more complex than these claims imply. While the prison improved upon existing carceral conditions, the administration still mistreated inmates and relied upon damaging practices like solitary confinement. Lyon’s narrative reflects this grim reality.

When Robert Smith designed Walnut Street Jail in 1773, prison conditions were objectively deplorable (Benson). Many incarcerated people experienced sexual violence, aggression, and harsh environments, making the prison a dangerous site; women and children, who were imprisoned in the same spaces as adult men, were particularly at risk (Benson). Yet by 1798, when Lyon was imprisoned, the prison and justice system had taken a different shape. In 1786, Pennsylvania reduced the list of capital offenses, so most incarcerated people were sentenced to hard public labor and nights in Walnut Street Jail rather than death (Rubin). Though that system proved ineffective, reform efforts persisted. In fact, the Philadelphia Society for Alleviating the Miseries of Public Prisons was founded in 1787, the very next year (Roberts and Roberts). This group — influenced heavily by Quakerism, though still non-denominational — strove to redefine the prison as a site of penance and personal improvement, not solely punishment (Michel; Roberts and Roberts). To them, shifting to private labor and embracing solitary confinement for noncapital offenders seemed like the best method of accomplishing this goal and deterring other citizens from committing crimes (Rubin). Motivated by these ideas, the prison was renovated in 1790 to include large common rooms, workshops, and 16 new solitary cells (Rubin). Its new name — Walnut Street Prison — was a reminder of this evolution.

Though official accounts romanticize these reforms, Lyon’s writing suggests that Walnut Street Prison was an unwholesome environment. In a 1794 document, Walnut Street administrator Caleb Lownes frames the prison as a model for others to follow. He argues that its focus on cleanliness, restoration, and decency granted humanity to incarcerated people during an era when many questioned their rights to light, air, and other basic needs (Lownes 10–14). South Carolina lawyer and political writer Robert J. Turnbull shared similar remarks after visiting Walnut Street Prison in 1796 (Krohn 3). He praised the prison’s “wise and humane administration,” emphasis on prisoners’ health, and relatively lenient punishments (Turnbull 1–25). However, Lyon’s experience implies that these claims might be exaggerated. He characterized the prison as “loathsome and unwholesome” and experienced brutal conditions (Lyon 26; Powell 81). Yellow fever raged throughout the prison, he nearly starved, his cell lacked a bed, and his interactions with others were censored (Powell 81; Lyon 44). All he had was a Bible, a book of poems, and basic writing materials (Powell 81). To get out, Lyon would have to raise $150,000 in bail, a sum that paralleled the amount stolen and would be difficult, if not impossible, for him to accumulate — especially since all but one of his friends had turned against him after hearing the accusations (Lyon 28–31). Despite his innocence, freedom felt unattainable.

Lyon’s imprisonment had physical and psychological consequences, alluding to the detrimental impacts of incarceration. These effects were visible: Lyon’s hair fell out, he lost a dangerous amount of weight, and he contracted yellow fever (Avery; Conway 31–41; Lyon 25). Yet they were also internal. While Lyon “acted as a gentleman since [he] was put in prison… [he] now found [his] temper could no longer be restrained” and even “told [Stocker] never more to expect a civil answer from [him]” (Lyon 29). After several weeks of incarceration, Lyon lost control over his emotions and behavior. This shift emphasizes the psychological impact of his imprisonment, but it also had dangerous potential consequences in a justice system that prized gentility and status over authenticity (Lyon 3–4). Lyon’s mental health only worsened as his incarceration lengthened. He grew suicidal, writing that the “the sooner [his life was] shortened the better” (Lyon 38). For him, death was preferable to staying in prison — especially since he knew was innocent and did not belong there. Lyon’s crumbling mental health reflects the adverse impact of his incarceration, but also mirrors broader trends within prison literature. Many incarcerated writers, including George Wilkerson and Judith Vazquez, emphasize the psychological harm of their imprisonment. Lyon’s experiences are not an isolated instance, but instead reflects ongoing systemic issues.

Though Lyon struggled throughout his incarceration, he clung to a few practices to keep himself afloat. He tried to distract himself while in solitary confinement — each day, he “read until [he] was tired, and walked till [he] was weary,” doing his best to pass the time before he was released (Lyon 25). Lyon also relied on religion throughout his imprisonment. He had a Bible in his cell and “frequently prayed in the most fervent manner… that the Great Creator might confuse those who had the money, so as the whole might be brought to light — to liberate the innocent, and bring to condign punishment the perpetrators of the deed, for the commission of which I was suffering” (Lyon 25). Most of the people he knew had abandoned him, yet he trusted that he was not truly alone. Instead, he believed that he had a divine ally, committed not only to his well-being but to restoring justice after the robbery. His faith helped him survive his incarceration, a common theme across ancient and contemporary prison literature. Though reading, exercise, and religion offered comfort and diversion during his imprisonment, they were also some of the only strategies he could embrace. Whistling, singing, and communicating with others all warranted punishment — he would be sent to the dungeon if he did so (Lyon 25). Those limitations compounded his challenging circumstances, making his incarceration even more damaging. 

Lyon’s innocence was his strongest anchor throughout his incarceration. Despite the challenges he endured, “[his] heart was good and firm: [he] knew and [he] felt [his] innocence, and that was [his] grand, great, and substantial support” (Lyon 38). His innocence was a source of comfort and reminded him that he still had integrity within his horrendous circumstances. It also motivated his writing. Lyon wanted to tell the truth about his incarceration, and documenting his experience allowed him to assert his innocence rather than have his story manipulated by bank authorities or other individuals with power. Yet his desire to write stemmed beyond self-preservation. He described the truth as his “mistress,” so although he knew that his work might upset others, he followed “her dictates, and penned what [he] did” (Lyon 30). For Lyon, recording the truth was a higher obligation; he was dedicated to the process itself, not just clearing his name. That commitment to justice helped him endure his incarceration and its aftermath.

###INJUSTICE AND EXONERATION

Carpenter Isaac Davis eventually confessed to robbing the Bank of Pennsylvania. Though Lyon did not know Davis’ name, he had suspected him for weeks — Davis was the stranger who had been drinking with Robinson (Lyon 62). He and Thomas Cunningham, a bank porter who died of yellow fever, had both worked for the Bank of Pennsylvania and coordinated the heist (Avery). Davis was discovered after depositing large sums of money across Philadelphia banks, including the very bank he stole from (Avery). This information did not exonerate Lyon, though — rather, “resentment had not yet quitted that malignant breast of some secret enemy… [he] had in that bank” (47). Though evidence pointed to his innocence, Lyon’s not-so-secret enemies — Fox, Stocker, and Smith, the bank cashier — continued to target him. They remained suspicious toward Lyon and accused him of having a set of false keys, leaving him in prison for several more weeks (Lyon 48). 

Fox, Stocker, and Smith’s resistance to exonerating Lyon was grounded in self-preservation, not genuine suspicion. During a conversation with one of Lyon’s friends, Smith admitted that keeping Lyon imprisoned shielded these authorities from the effects of their mishaps (Lyon 55–57). He, Stocker, and Fox planned to “use him worse” and were “not done with him yet”; instead, they wanted to “try and confine him longer” (Lyon 55). They had Lyon “bound down, and… fixed so that he [could not] sue [them]” (Lyon 57). Smith’s remarks reveal that he and his counterparts were willing and able to exploit their power to maintain their status — even if they knew that they were harming an innocent man in the process. Self-interest, rather than the pursuit of truth, motivated their actions. 

Despite Fox, Stocker, and Smith’s efforts, Lyon was exonerated. After Davis wrote a letter alleging Lyon’s innocence, his bail was lowered to $2,000 — a sum he could afford — and a grand jury cleared his name in January 1799 (Avery). Lyon had spent over three months in Walnut Street Prison, but was finally free.

###THE IRONY OF INNOCENCE

Lyon was frustrated by the power dynamics and exploitation present throughout his legal process, and those sentiments echo throughout his work. He opens his narrative with a two-page message to the public, encouraging them to recognize that poor, innocent men are often unfairly accused while wealthier, guilty individuals can escape punishment (Lyon 3–4). Lyon writes that “every one ought to have his full portion of justice,” but questions the attainability of this equity (Lyon 3). In his experience, “the rich can elude justice by giving great bail, and feeing lawyers high, who by that and other methods, protract the affair till the action dies away by one means or another” (Lyon 3). Conversely, “if the small fry get in the least entramelled in the meshes of the law, they are generally fastened in the net, and often times punished wrongfully” (Lyon 3). He presents wealthy and poor individuals’ differing outcomes as a resource disparity — the rich have the money to effectively outrun their greatest missteps, while the poor are trapped by even the smallest accusations. Lyon calls attention to structural inequality within the justice system before his story even begins; in doing so, he helps readers identify how it impacted his own incarceration experience and emphasizes the urgency of the issue.

{% include feature/image.html objectid="95" width="75" caption="The first page of Lyon's message to the public." %}

Lyon makes the disparities in the justice system evident at the start of his work and offers concrete examples of how they materialize throughout the rest of his narrative. Early in his imprisonment, he asked Stocker how he could get out of prison. Stocker told him to hire a lawyer “(whether [he] had money or not [he] suppose[d]), and be removed by habeas corpus before any of the Judges, and get out on bail” (Lyon 28). While Stocker might have presented the best course of action, he also relied on a flawed assumption: that Lyon had the money to hire an excellent lawyer and pay his exorbitant bail. His directions illuminate the inequitable value of money in the justice system. In Stocker’s eyes and colonial reality, wealth — not innocence — was the most feasible path to freedom. 

While prosperity helped some individuals escape their accusations, connections and reputation were just as valuable. When Isaac Davis confessed to robbing the Bank of Pennsylvania, he had minimal consequences (Avery). As the nephew of a powerful judge, he was pardoned and fled Philadelphia (Hunt). Personal networks allowed the guilty Davis to escape punishment, while the innocent — but unconnected — Lyon remained in Walnut Street Prison. However, relationships and money were not the only means of evading justice. According to Lyon, stereotypes and prejudice informed accusations and outcomes too. In his experience, “mankind may mistake on the principles of physiognomy, for many a man, with a fine appearance, has deserved the gallows, and many a valuable diamond, has been unjustly punished, because he had a rough outside” (Lyon 48). His words illustrate a paradoxical reality: morality and guilt are not externally legible, but many people assume otherwise. These dynamics informed the justice system in Lyon’s time and complicated the pursuit of truth; they also led to unjust outcomes, like Lyon’s incarceration.

These inequities still persist in the American justice system over 200 years later. Many innocent people have been incarcerated and even sentenced to death (“Death Penalty”). Several factors can lead to this injustice, like false or coerced confessions, misleading evidence, official misconduct, and perjury (“Death Penalty”). Like Lyon describes, money is a major element of these errors as well. Poor individuals might not have the resources to pay for adequate legal defense, leaving them vulnerable to incarceration despite their innocence (“Death Penalty”). Another part of Lyon’s claim still holds true: systemic bias in the justice system. As Michelle Alexander explains in The New Jim Crow, Black and Hispanic individuals are incarcerated at disproportionately high rates and often subject to more extreme punishment. Though these groups make up just 31% of the U.S. population, they account for 53% of death row inmates and are more likely to be prosecuted in general (“Race and the Death Penalty”; “Death Penalty”). Those statistics reinforce both Lyon’s and Alexander’s points, reminding readers that the justice system still privileges certain groups.

While Lyon attempted to draw attention to these structural inequalities and countless contemporary writers have done the same — including Angela Davis and Ruth Wilson Gilmore — they still echo throughout the American justice system. Money, status, and stereotypes all seem to have more weight than the truth itself, meaning that many innocent people are wrongly incarcerated. Despite its name, the justice system relies upon and exacerbates societal injustice — an ironic reality that we still struggle to escape.

###LEGACIES OF INNOCENCE

Even after being released from Walnut Street Prison, Lyon continued to advocate for his innocence and the truth. As part of that process, he published his narrative later in 1799 (Lyon 6). Not only did he want to share his own story, but he wanted to call attention to the ways the justice system privileges the wealthiest while simultaneously punishing “those who are inferior in rank,” like him (Lyon 3). His message was powerful, and it resonated: Lyon’s story was well-known throughout Philadelphia and was even used as a moral tale for children (Patrick 189). Though Lyon had never envisioned himself as a writer — he was much “more accustomed to mechanics” — his incarceration experience changed that narrative (Lyon 75).

Although Lyon's work became popular, he still struggled for the next several years. His reputation suffered, and he had to live off of his friends and  “in poverty and disgrace” until 1805, when his lawyer Alexander J. Dallas brought suit for malicious prosecution against Bank of Pennsylvania authorities (Patrick 189). After four hours of deliberation, he received $12,000 in damages for wrongful imprisonment (later appealed and lowered to $9,000) (Lloyd 183-184). When the verdict was announced, a “universal clamor of exultation took place among the audience, the most numerous the reporter remembers ever to have assembled in that court room” (Lloyd 183). People across the city were invested in Lyon’s freedom, suggesting that his innocence was finally recognized to its fullest extent. 

Lyon went on to be objectively successful. He had a career as a fire engine manufacturer and became a “prosperous Philadelphia citizen, large, sturdy, full fashioned, with a strong mind and a taste for strong beer” (Chambers 228; Patrick 189). By the time he died in 1829, he was “advantageously known to [Philadelphia] citizens,” publicly recognized as “honest,” and “long withdrawn from the occupations of trade” (“Patrick Lyon, the Hydraulic Engine Maker”; “Patrick Lyon”).

Despite that evolution, he never escaped Walnut Street Prison for good. Even when he commissioned two life-sized — and therefore exorbitantly expensive — portraits of himself by artist John Neagle, he requested to be shown in the smithy with Walnut Street Prison in the background (Chambers 225-227; Rigal 179-181; Neagle 1829; Neagle 1826-1827). He refused to let go of who he was before and during the trial, an innocent working man who had been unjustly accused by the upper class. Though it had been nearly thirty years since his imprisonment, the experience still haunted him and his self-perception — a reminder of the indelible harm that can and does happen in the carceral state.
